# Comms Improver: FTC Complaint Excerpts — Jurisdiction and Prior Order Violations

**Audience:** Business executive briefing
**Purpose:** Summarizing the legal context and allegations from an FTC complaint for a non-legal executive reader
**Length constraint:** Matched to original (compressed for executive briefing)
**Tone:** Crisp, neutral, decision-oriented

## Rewritten text

> **Background: Rite Aid's prior FTC order and what the government says went wrong**
>
> Rite Aid has been under a 2010 FTC order (*In the Matter of Rite Aid Corporation, C-4308, 150 F.T.C. 694 (Nov. 12, 2010)*) since it was found to have violated *Section 5(a) of the FTC Act*. That order required Rite Aid to build and maintain a serious information security program and to keep records proving it was doing so.
>
> The FTC now alleges Rite Aid broke those commitments in three specific ways:
> - **Vendor oversight:** Rite Aid didn't take reasonable steps to check whether vendors handling its customers' personal information were actually protecting that data, and didn't require those protections in contracts.
> - **Record-keeping:** Rite Aid failed to keep written records documenting its information security program.
> - **Withheld documents:** Rite Aid failed to hand over documents when the FTC asked — including documents that contradicted or undermined its claims of compliance.
>
> **Why this court:** The FTC filed in this federal district court because Rite Aid is headquartered here, does business here, and the relevant conduct occurred here. Federal jurisdiction is based on *28 U.S.C. §§ 1331, 1337(a), and 1345* and *15 U.S.C. § 53(b)*; venue is established under *28 U.S.C. §§ 1391(b)(1), (b)(2), (c)(2), and (d)*.

## What was changed

- **Led with the bottom line.** The original buries the substance in procedural framing. The rewrite opens with what the 2010 order required and what Rite Aid allegedly did wrong, which is what an executive needs first.
- **Bullet structure for the three alleged violations.** The original runs the three failures together in a long compound paragraph. Bullets make the distinct allegations immediately readable.
- **Jurisdiction compressed to a single sentence.** The jurisdictional paragraphs (8 and 9) are largely procedural boilerplate — important for the legal record, of little relevance to executive decision-making. The rewrite gives a one-sentence explanation of *why this court* and preserves the statutory citations verbatim as specified.
- **Active voice throughout.** "Rite Aid didn't take reasonable steps" instead of "Rite Aid routinely failed to use reasonable steps in selecting and retaining service providers capable of appropriately safeguarding…"

## Preservation-of-meaning concerns

**One substantive concern worth flagging:**

The original alleges that Rite Aid failed to produce documents "that contradict, qualify, or call into question its compliance." This is a significant allegation — it suggests document suppression, not just poor record-keeping. The rewrite captures this ("including documents that contradicted or undermined its claims of compliance") but the executive should understand this is among the more serious of the three failure categories, potentially indicating bad faith rather than mere administrative sloppiness. If this briefing is being used to advise on litigation posture or communications strategy, that distinction matters and should be surfaced explicitly.

**One structural note:**

The original text is excerpts from a formal court complaint — paragraphs 7, 8, and 9. The rewrite treats them as a unified briefing block. If the executive audience needs to know this is taken from a formal legal filing (e.g., because the briefing will reference the public complaint), a one-line framing note before the rewrite would be appropriate: *"The following is drawn from the FTC's federal court complaint."*
