# Article 9.2 objection map

The grounds on which a tribunal may exclude a document from production or evidence, under the IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence (2020), Article 9.2. The producing party maps each request against this menu. The skill frames and records the objection. It does not decide whether the objection holds. That is the tribunal's call.

This file paraphrases the rule and cites article numbers. It does not reproduce the rule text. Confirm the official sub-numbering and wording against the IBA PDF before relying on it in a filing.

## The seven grounds: Article 9.2(a) to (g)

- **(a) Relevance or materiality.** The request is not sufficiently relevant to the case or material to its outcome. The first filter, and the answer to a fishing expedition or an "all documents relating to" sweep.
- **(b) Legal impediment or privilege.** Barred under the legal or ethical rules the tribunal decides apply. Sub-types to pick from:
  - attorney-client or legal-advice privilege,
  - litigation privilege or work product,
  - without-prejudice or settlement privilege,
  - in-house counsel privilege (jurisdiction-dependent, recognised in common-law systems, restricted in many civil-law and EU contexts, flag the jurisdiction),
  - deliberative or governmental-process privilege (jurisdiction-dependent and not universally recognised, like in-house privilege, so assert it under (b) only where a specific domestic state-secrecy or official-information statute is named, and route the political or institutional sensitivity of governmental deliberation primarily through ground (f) below),
  - other legal impediment (statutory or professional secrecy, banking secrecy, data-protection or GDPR constraints, state secrecy).
  State the asserted rules, the jurisdiction, and the factual basis. When a (b) objection is asserted, record the privilege type, the governing jurisdiction or jurisdictions, and the Article 9.4 factors in play, and prepare a privilege log. Where the parties sit in different privilege regimes, flag the cross-regime question (see Article 9.4 below).
- **(c) Unreasonable burden.** Producing the requested evidence is unreasonably burdensome. A bare assertion fails. A real objection gives specifics: volume, cost, custodian count, systems to be searched.
- **(d) Loss or destruction.** Shown with reasonable likelihood, not bare assertion.
- **(e) Commercial or technical confidentiality.** Trade secrets, pricing, proprietary technical data. The tribunal must find the confidentiality compelling. Usually met by redaction or a confidentiality ring rather than refusal.
- **(f) Special political or institutional sensitivity.** Including material a government or a public international institution has classified as secret. The tribunal must find the sensitivity compelling. See the state-party note below.
- **(g) Procedural economy, proportionality, fairness, or equality.** The compelling-considerations backstop. The home for proportionality and for electronic requests that are disproportionate to their value.

## Supporting provisions

Note the 2020 renumbering. The 2020 revision inserted a new provision on illegally obtained evidence as the new **9.3**, which moved the rest down by one against the 2010 text. **Verified against the official 2020 IBA PDF (ibanet.org) on 2026-06-20:** 9.3 is illegally obtained evidence, 9.4 is the privilege-determination factors (Article 9.2(b) itself cross-references "see Article 9.4 below"), 9.5 is the protective-measures provision, and 9.6 and 9.7 are the adverse-inference provisions (documents and other evidence). As ordinary diligence, confirm against the edition in force in your arbitration.

- **9.4: privilege-determination factors.** When ruling on a 9.2(b) claim the tribunal may weigh: the need to protect the confidentiality of legal advice, protection of settlement-related documents, the parties' expectations at the time, any waiver, and the need to maintain fairness and equality where the parties sit in different privilege regimes. Article 9.4 lists factors. It is not a choice-of-law rule. Where the parties sit in different privilege regimes, tribunals resolve the asymmetry in competing ways: the most-protective or maximalist approach (apply the broader privilege to both), the least-protective approach, and closest-connection or most-significant-relationship approaches. There is no settled rule. Identify each party's regime, flag the asymmetry, and leave the choice of approach to counsel.
- **9.5: protective measures the producing party can offer.** Even where production is ordered, confidentiality can be preserved short of full disclosure by measures under which the requesting side's counsel still receives the document under protection: redaction (clearly marked, with the basis stated), confidentiality rings or clubs, and restricted access. Pair grounds (b), (e), and (f) with a 9.5 option rather than a flat refusal. Note the boundary: in-camera or tribunal-only review, where only the tribunal sees the document, is a tribunal mechanism, not a 9.5 measure the producing party can offer. Frame it as something the producing party invites the tribunal to order, not as a measure it grants.
- **9.6 and 9.7: adverse inferences.** The tribunal may infer that a withheld document, or other withheld evidence, would be unfavourable to a party that fails, without satisfactory explanation, to produce it. The inference ordinarily requires either a request the party did not object to, or a tribunal order to produce, that the party then failed to satisfy, rather than any unanswered request. In practice the inference attaches to material the tribunal regards as relevant and material, but treat that as a practical tendency, not a fixed precondition.

## State or state-owned party: the (f) sensitivity prompt

When any party is marked a State or a state-owned entity, surface ground (f) as a candidate for the producing party's consideration. Do not auto-assert it on every request. Ownership of the party does not by itself make a document politically or institutionally sensitive. Recommend asserting (f) only where the document's **content** plausibly implicates a governmental or sovereign function: ministry or cabinet material, classified or national-security material, deliberative governmental documents, public-interest immunity. On the ordinary commercial documents of a state-owned enterprise (board minutes about a commercial decision, ordinary contracts, invoices), (f) is weak to inapt, and the candidate prompt should say so rather than dress a commercial document as a state secret.

Ground (f) is the one ground tailored to public actors. Its express reach is special political or institutional sensitivity, including material a government or a public international institution has classified as secret. In practice tribunals also weigh deliberative or governmental sensitivity, national security, and public-interest immunity under this head, but those are practitioner gloss on what institutional sensitivity can reach, not separately enumerated grounds in the rule.

Carve-out: do not raise (f) on a request that is already disposed of on relevance or materiality (a) or on burden (c). Adding a sensitivity objection to a request that fails on relevance adds nothing. Note it in the internal flags memo rather than the schedule.

Two limits to state plainly:
- The tribunal independently decides whether the sensitivity is compelling. A State cannot self-certify a blanket exemption by pointing to its own classification law.
- The State must still search, identify, and substantively justify each document's status. Ground (f) is not a veto.

Where the content does warrant it, pair the (f) objection with a 9.5 producing-party measure (redaction, restricted access) and, separately, with an invitation to the tribunal to order in-camera review where only the tribunal need see the document. The 9.5 measures are what the producing party offers. In-camera review is what it asks the tribunal to order.

What counts as State or state-owned, to guide the user's toggle:
- the State itself, or an organ or ministry,
- a public international institution,
- a state-owned enterprise (majority state ownership, or state control such as a golden share),
- an entity exercising delegated governmental or sovereign authority.
Treat majority ownership or effective control as triggering the flag. Let the user record the basis. The flag arms a prompt. It asserts nothing.

## How the grounds feed the output

- Each objection goes into the *Objections* column with its ground letter, a one-line basis, and, for (b), (e), and (f), the protective-measure option offered.
- The producing party owns the *Objections* column only. Every other column is reproduced verbatim.
- The skill does not weigh whether the objection will succeed. It records the ground and the basis so the tribunal and counsel can.

## Internal flags memo for the producing party

The producing party gets the same honest internal check the requesting party gets. Alongside the schedule, produce a short internal flags memo, marked privileged and never sent to the other side or the tribunal, naming the producing party's own weak or non-colourable objections so the user can decide whether to press or drop them. Flag, for example:
- ground (f) asserted on a plainly commercial document where the content does not implicate a governmental function,
- a bare ground (c) burden assertion with no volume, cost, custodian count, or systems named,
- a blanket privilege claim under (b) with no privilege type, jurisdiction, or Article 9.4 grounding and no privilege log,
- an objection piled onto a request already answered on relevance or burden, which adds nothing.

The memo describes the weakness. It does not drop the objection. The user decides.
